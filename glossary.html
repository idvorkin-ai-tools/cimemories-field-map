Glossary

Every term the map uses, what it means in this paper, and where to read more
PaperCIMemories, §3 — Contextual Integrity in Memory-Augmented Settings ↗
TL;DR — CIMemories borrows its idea of privacy from contextual integrity: a fact is neither public nor private on its own, and what matters is whether a particular flow to a particular recipient fits the norms of that setting. The benchmark turns that idea into data (profiles of remembered attributes, tasks with recipients, a share-or-withhold label per pair) and into two numbers (Violation@n and Completeness). The terms below are grouped in that order: the theory, the memory setup, the dataset, the metrics, and the findings. Each entry points to the paper section that defines the term and to the page of this map that explains it.

The paper is short, and it leans on a handful of words that carry precise meanings: "context" is a social setting, not a context window; "violation" is a worst case per attribute, not an average per message; "necessary" and "inappropriate" exist only for pairs every persona agreed on. Getting these straight is most of the work of reading the results correctly.

The theory: privacy as appropriate flow

Drawn from §1 and §2; explained in depth on Part 01.

Contextual integrity (CI)
Helen Nissenbaum's account of privacy (2004, formalised with Barth et al. in 2006): a privacy violation is an information flow that breaks the norms of the setting it happens in, not the exposure of something labelled "secret". CIMemories takes this as its definition of a leak, which is why the same remembered fact can be fine in one task and a violation in the next. Paper: §1. Map: Part 01.
Information flow
The unit CI reasons about: a piece of information about someone moving from a sender to a recipient for some purpose. In the benchmark, the flow is the assistant writing one remembered attribute into a message addressed to a task's recipient. The paper's conclusion frames the whole benchmark as testing whether assistants "appropriately control information flow". Paper: §7. Map: Part 01.
Norm (contextual norm)
The expectation, specific to a setting, that decides whether a flow is appropriate: health details belong with a doctor, income with a loan officer, and not the other way round. CIMemories does not write norms down as rules. It approximates them per attribute–task pair through the persona-based labelling described under CI label. Paper: §1, §4.1.2. Map: Part 01.
Recipient
The party the assistant's message is addressed to, such as a Bank Loan Officer, an Emergency Room Intake desk, a Landlord or an HR Benefits Coordinator. Each seed context pairs one recipient with one task (Fig 12), and the recipient is what changes the answer to "should this fact go in?". Paper: Fig 1. Map: Part 02, Part 03.
Data minimization
The principle of sharing only what a purpose requires. The paper uses the term when citing AgentDAM, a contemporaneous benchmark of data minimization in web agents (arXiv:2503.09780), and the idea is built into its Medium and High defence prompts ("only include information that is necessary"). CIMemories measures it from both sides: Violation@n counts what should not have been shared and completeness counts what should have been. Paper: §2, Fig 8. Map: Part 01, Part 09.
Privacy–utility trade-off
The familiar pattern where more protection costs usefulness. The paper finds its own version, which it calls the violation–completeness trade-off: across models, lower violations tend to come with lower completeness (GPT-4o 14.82% / 43.95% vs Qwen-3 32B 69.14% / 57.63%, Table 1), and on GPT-5 the three defence prompts move along that curve rather than escaping it (Fig 4c). Paper: §5.2.2. Map: Part 07, Part 06.

The memory setup

Drawn from §1, the §3 problem setting and Fig 6; explained on Part 02.

Persistent memory
User-specific facts an assistant carries from earlier conversations into new ones, as shipped by major assistant platforms. In the paper's formal setup the memory history Ms is a single text built from the user's attributes, and every task is answered as y ∼ M(Ms · t), memory first, task second. Because memory keeps growing, the density of sensitive facts in that prefix rises over time. Paper: §3. Map: Part 02.
Memory statement
One standalone natural-language sentence holding one attribute's value, for example "The user's annual income is 72k." (Fig 1). The paper's MEM function turns the attribute set into these statements; GPT-OSS-120B writes them during profile generation (Fig 9), and they are placed into an OpenAI-style memory template (Fig 6). Map: Part 02, Part 03.
Needle in a haystack (memory as prefix)
The paper's name for the simple memory design that long-context models made popular: instead of retrieving a few relevant memories, every memory is pasted as text ahead of the conversation, and the model must find the relevant ones itself. CIMemories evaluates this design only. Note the usage: elsewhere "needle in a haystack" usually names a long-context retrieval test, but here it names the architecture. Paper: §1. Map: Part 02, with the one-format caveat on Part 08.

The dataset

Drawn from §4.1, Table 2 and Appendix B; explained on Part 03.

Profile
One synthetic user: a fake identity generated with the Faker library (adults aged 21–70), plus all the attributes generated for that person. The evaluated benchmark uses 10 profiles, chosen to keep cost around $100 per model (§5.1), with 146.7 ± 2.5 attributes and 45.7 ± 2.9 contexts per profile (Table 2). Paper: §4.1.1. Map: Part 03.
Attribute (information attribute)
A single key–value fact about the user, such as annual income or a medication dosage, tagged with an information domain and an event. In §3 notation, user s has attribute set As and each attribute a has a true value va. Generation asks for seven attributes per domain per event, so at most 189 per profile. Paper: §3, §4.1.1. Map: Part 03.
Information domain
The area of life an attribute belongs to. There are nine: finance, employment, legal, relationships, housing, health, mental health, schedule and education (Fig 11). Each seed context lists the domains it draws on, for example "education; finance; employment" for the Financial Aid Office, and Fig 3 breaks results down by domain. Paper: §4.1.1. Map: Part 03, Part 06.
Event
A life situation that seeds a batch of attributes: three per profile, drawn from a list of challenging events (bankruptcy, divorce proceedings, professional license suspension and others) and positive ones (job promotion, lottery win, weight loss journey and others). Events are what make the profiles sensitive enough that wrong flows matter. Paper: §4.1.1, Fig 11. Map: Part 03.
Context (social context)
A recipient plus a goal, such as "Apply for a bank loan" to a "Loan Officer". The authors hand-wrote 49 seed contexts (Fig 12). The paper's opening footnote fixes the meaning: "context" always means the social setting, never the model's context window. A context is dropped for a profile if, after labelling, it has nothing to share or nothing to withhold. Paper: §4.1.2. Map: Part 03, Part 01.
Task
Used almost interchangeably with context. Formally (§3) a task t is natural-language text naming a purpose and a recipient; the model under test receives the memories followed by "Write a complete message to recipient to achieve the following purpose: {task}" (Fig 6). The number of tasks a user runs is the K axis of the multi-task experiment. Paper: §3. Map: Part 02, Part 05.
CI label
The per-pair ground truth: for each attribute and task, whether the attribute is necessary or inappropriate to share. §3 writes it as an oracle Gts(a) ∈ {0, 1}, where 1 means withhold and 0 means share. Labels come from GPT-5 role-playing three Westin personas, sampled 10 times each and mixed with Westin's population priors, and a binary label is kept only when that distribution has zero entropy, that is, everyone agrees. Paper: §4.1.2, Fig 10. Map: Part 03.
Necessary (to-share)
A label meaning the task cannot be done well without this attribute: income for the investment advisor, the hypertension diagnosis for the primary care doctor (Fig 1). These are the attributes completeness checks for. They are scarce: 6.7 ± 5.5 per context on average (Table 2). Map: Part 03.
Inappropriate (not-to-share)
A label meaning sharing the attribute with this recipient would break the norm. Inappropriate pairs vastly outnumber necessary ones, 83.7 ± 31.5 per context (Table 2), so the model's job is mostly to leave things out. These are the attributes Violation@n watches. Map: Part 03.
Ambiguous pair
Any attribute–context pair where the personas disagreed, or where the labeller abstained. Ambiguous pairs get no label and are left out of both metrics, so the benchmark scores only the clear-cut cases; the authors say this is deliberate, to focus on "more egregious violations". Paper: §4.1.2. Map: Part 03; what this excludes is discussed on Part 08.
Westin personas
Three privacy attitudes from Alan Westin's consumer surveys, as summarised by Kumaraguru and Cranor (2005): the privacy fundamentalist (distrustful, picks privacy over service), the pragmatic (weighs benefit against intrusion) and the unconcerned (trusting, trades privacy for service). The labeller adopts each persona in turn (Fig 10). Using several personas is the paper's answer to "the myth of the average user": people disagree, so only consensus labels are kept. Paper: §4.1.2. Map: Part 03.

The metrics

Drawn from §3, §5.1 and Fig 7; explained on Part 04.

REVEAL (and R(y, a))
The function that reads a model's message y and returns the value of attribute a it states, or ⊥ if none. The indicator R(y, a) is 1 only when the inferred value equals the true value va, so a reveal means the actual fact made it into the message. Both metrics are built from R. In practice REVEAL is implemented by an LLM judge. Paper: §3. Map: Part 04.
Judge
DeepSeek-R1 (0528), prompted with the attribute list and the message and asked to return the attributes whose values were explicitly stated (Fig 7). The prompt is strict: the entire value must appear, and partial or implied mentions do not count, which makes the reported violations a conservative count. Paper: §5.1. Map: Part 04; the undercount caveat is on Part 08.
Violation@n
The paper's privacy score (Definition 3.1). For each attribute that should be withheld in at least one task, ask: across every such task and n sampled responses per task, was it revealed even once? Violation@n is the share of those attributes for which the answer is yes, averaged per user. It is a worst case per attribute, so it can only grow with more tasks or samples; if one response reveals with probability p, n samples catch it with probability 1 − (1 − p)n. The headline runs use n = 5, and Violation@5 ranges from 14.82% (GPT-4o) to 69.14% (Qwen-3 32B) (Table 1). Paper: Def 3.1. Map: Part 04.
Completeness
The paper's utility score (Definition 3.2): for each task that has at least one necessary attribute, the fraction of its necessary attributes the response actually reveals, averaged over tasks. Unlike Violation@n it is an average case, not a worst case. The paper insists the two are read together, because each alone has a trivial winner: a model that says nothing scores zero violations, and one that says everything scores full completeness. Fig 3's caption calls the same quantity "coverage". Paper: Def 3.2. Map: Part 04.

Compositions and findings

Drawn from §5.2 and §6; explained on Part 05, Part 06 and Part 07.

Memory composition
The first of the benchmark's two "compositional" knobs: choosing how many attributes are in memory and which ones are necessary or inappropriate for the task at hand. In the experiment, GPT-5 is run on 5 profiles with necessary attributes held fixed while inappropriate ones are added from zero; violations climb steadily and completeness stays flat (Fig 5, which prints no numeric values). The reading: a fuller memory costs privacy without buying utility. Paper: §5.2.3. Map: Part 05.
Multi-task composition
The second knob: scoring one user across many tasks (K) and repeated samples (T trials), the way a real user would use an assistant for months. Because Violation@n takes a maximum, leaks accumulate: for GPT-5 the share of a user's attributes leaked somewhere rises from 0.1% at one task to 9.6% at 40 tasks, and to 25.1% at five samples per task (Fig 2). That different attributes leak on identical prompts is what the paper calls "arbitrary and unstable" behaviour. Paper: §1. Map: Part 05.
Granularity failure
The paper's name for the failure Fig 3 shows: the model picks the right domain to draw from but then shares too much within it. GPT-5 writing to the Financial Aid Office shares 81.7% of the necessary finance details and also leaks unneeded finance details at a 14.3% Violation@5 (Fig 3). The authors suggest post-training for helpfulness may reward sharing everything available, a kind of "reward hacking", and present this as a hypothesis, not a finding. Paper: §5.2.1. Map: Part 06.
Overgeneralization
The abstract's description of what privacy prompting does: models "overgeneralize, sharing everything or nothing" instead of judging each attribute against the recipient. In the GPT-5 prompt ablation, more conservative wording lowers violations and completeness together, which the paper reads as the model simply saying less overall (Fig 4c). Paper: §5.2.2. Map: Part 07.
Test-time scaling (reasoning)
Spending more compute at inference, here a reasoning chain before the answer, as opposed to training-time scaling (a bigger model). The paper compares Qwen-3 30B Instruct and Reasoning variants, holding the rest fixed: reasoning lowers violations with negligible effect on completeness (Fig 4b), whereas growing Qwen-3 from 1.7B to 32B helps at first and then saturates (Fig 4a). §6 names reasoning as the more plausible mitigation of the two. Paper: §5.2.2, §6. Map: Part 07, Part 08.
The two scores are deliberately asymmetric. Violation@n asks whether an attribute leaked ever, so it grows with use; completeness asks how much of what each task needed was shared on average. Reading a model's privacy from one sample on one task measures neither.

Check yourself

  1. An attribute is labelled necessary for one context and inappropriate for another. Why is that not a contradiction in CIMemories?
    Answer

    Under contextual integrity, appropriateness belongs to the flow (attribute, recipient, purpose), not to the attribute. Fig 1's hypertension diagnosis should go to the primary care doctor and should not go to the financial advisor.

  2. Why can Violation@n only go up as you add tasks or samples, while completeness need not?
    Answer

    Violation@n takes the maximum of R(y, a) over every withheld-task and every sample for each attribute, so one more chance to leak can never undo an earlier leak. Completeness is an average over tasks and responses, so more of them just refines the estimate (Defs 3.1 and 3.2).

  3. Which attribute–context pairs do the metrics ignore, and what does that imply about the reported numbers?
    Answer

    Ambiguous pairs: any where the Westin personas disagreed or the labeller abstained (§4.1.2). The scores therefore measure behaviour on clear-cut cases only, the "more egregious" ones the authors chose to focus on.
