Turn the dials that make a small per-message leak rate into a large per-user one, and compare the model to the paper's measured points.

CIMemories' headline curve (Figure 2) runs GPT-5 through more and more tasks and counts how many of a user's should-be-private facts have leaked at least once. One task, one sample: 0.1%. Forty tasks: 9.6%. Forty tasks, five samples each: 25.1%. This page is a small model of why that happens. It is the map's model, not the paper's; the paper prints the three measured points and the curve's shape, and the point of the page is to see what assumptions do and do not reproduce them.

What the dials show

Samples multiply exposures. Violation@n is a worst case: a fact counts as leaked if it appears in any sample of any task where it was inappropriate (Part 04). If a fact is exposed eK times per sample and T samples are drawn, it survives all eKT exposures with probability (1 − p)eKT. Everything else on this page follows from that one line. Move T from 1 to 5 and watch the whole curve lift; that is the gap between Figure 2's 9.6% and 25.1%.

Independence gives a concave curve, and the paper's first point sits below it. With every fact alike, the model cannot go from 0.1% at one task to 9.6% at forty: forty times the exposure gives at most forty times the leakage when p is small, and the curve bends down, not up. Try the first experiment above. The honest reading is that the paper's single-task point is not an average task: which tasks a user runs matters as much as how many, and the paper does not say how its forty are ordered (Part 05). A benchmark of your own should randomise task order or report the spread.

A leak-prone minority explains the ceiling. Lower f and the curve flattens toward f: no matter how many tasks run, facts that never leak never leak. The Table 1 ticks on the right edge are eight models' Violation@5 over a full task set. Under this model each tick is a lower bound on that model's f: at least that share of a user's at-risk facts are the kind that leaks. Qwen-3 32B's 69.14% means more than two thirds of a user's facts leaked at least once in five draws over about 46 tasks. The paper does not estimate f; the dial is a way to think about what the number implies.

The other dial: more memory

Figure 2 varies tasks with memory fixed. Figure 5 does the reverse on five profiles: the facts a task needs stay in memory and inappropriate ones are added from zero. Violations rise while completeness stays flat. The paper prints the trend, not the values, so the panel below is the shape only, driven by the model above.

The flat line is the part to notice. Extra inappropriate facts buy nothing for the task, so completeness does not move, while each one is another draw against p. An assistant whose memory grows for months is walking up the x-axis of this panel. For builders, lesson 5 draws the conclusion: scope what memory a task can see.
