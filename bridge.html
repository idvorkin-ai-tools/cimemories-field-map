Two maps, one model

Where the CIMemories benchmark sits inside the CAPS report's framework, and the benchmark's words translated into the report's
TL;DR — The CAPS report says what an agent would have to do to respect contextual integrity: five reasoning steps, under four threat models. CIMemories measures one cell of that grid with care. It fixes the sender, the subject and the transmission principle, varies only the recipient and purpose, assumes no adversary, and asks whether a model applies a norm it was never shown. The report's own survey of benchmarks (Appendix D) codes it that way: simple contextualization, simple transmission principles, no designed ambiguity. Read the benchmark as the report's step 3 under its fourth threat model, and the two sets of numbers and problems line up.
CIMemories

A measurement. Ten synthetic people, about 147 remembered facts each, about 46 recipients each. Score: did a fact ever reach a recipient it was labelled inappropriate for (Violation@n), and did the needed facts arrive (Completeness).

CAPS report

A framework and a problem list. Privacy and security for agents as appropriateness in context; a five-step reasoning pipeline no agent can yet run; 51 open problems on how to build it, measure it and govern it.

The agentic privacy map · 10 minutes

The one model

Both documents stand on the same claim: a disclosure is wrong when it breaks the norms of the context it happens in, not when the fact is secret. The report states the theory in full and asks how a machine could enforce it. The benchmark takes one slice of the theory, turns it into labels, and measures how far today's models are from enforcing even that slice.

The report's version of the claim is the five-parameter flow: a sender passes information of some type, about a subject, to a recipient, under a transmission principle, and the context's norms say which such flows are appropriate (CI, applied to agents). The benchmark's version is the same tuple with three slots welded shut. The sender is always the assistant writing for the user, the subject is always the user, and every message travels under one principle, "what this task needs". What varies is the task, which brings a recipient and a purpose, and the fact, which is one remembered attribute (CI, from Nissenbaum to LLMs). A label on each (attribute, task) pair stands in for the norm.

Three consequences of that placement are worth holding onto.

  • The benchmark assumes the hardest part is done. Step 2, finding the norm, is what the report calls out as missing: there is no repository of contextual norms an agent could look up (OP 6.3, OP 12.2). CIMemories manufactures that repository for its 10 users by asking GPT-5 to play three privacy personas and keeping only the pairs they agree on. The model under test never sees those labels; it is scored on whether it behaves as if it knew them.
  • The benchmark's numbers are the no-adversary row. Nobody injects anything. The 14.8% to 69.1% violation rates in Table 1 are what the report means by agents that fail "through poor judgment or over-eagerness" with no attacker present. The report's other three rows, including prompt injection, are not measured by CIMemories at all.
  • Its two scores are the report's definition of a trustworthy agent, minus security. The report wants utility plus privacy plus security. Completeness is utility; Violation@n is privacy; security (actions that are not information flows) is out of scope because the benchmark's only action is writing a message.

Vocabulary concordance

The two documents name the same objects differently. The table reads left to right: the benchmark's word, the report's word, and what gets lost or gained in translation.

taskcontext + recipient + purposeA task like "apply for a personal loan [bank loan officer]" fixes the social context (finance), the recipient and the transmission principle at once. Appendix D codes this as "Simple" contextualization and "Simple" transmission principles.

Norms versus preferences. The report is strict about the difference: a norm is legitimate when it serves the purposes of the context, while a preference is what one person happens to want (CI, applied to agents). CIMemories builds its ground truth from three survey archetypes of individual preference, sampled from one model, and keeps the unanimous cases. Where all three temperaments agree, preference and norm plausibly coincide, which is the authors' argument for the unanimity rule. Where they do not, the pair is dropped, so the benchmark never adjudicates a norm against a preference. A product filter trained on these labels enforces aggregated preferences; the report's policy engine is meant to look up norms. Both are useful; they are not the same object.

How the report codes the benchmark

Appendix D of the report scores 16 contextual-integrity benchmarks on ten features. Its row for CIMemories, from Table 2, p. 116, cited as Mireshghallah et al. (2026):

FeatureCodingWhy, in the benchmark's own terms
ContextualizationSimpleA recipient and a one-line purpose; no personas or roles on the recipient side.

AmbiguityNoDisputed pairs are removed rather than designed in.

ExplanationNoLabels carry no justification to check a model's reasoning against.

The report's own reading of this table is in its evaluation section: current benchmarks are mostly atomic, synthetic and single-shot, which is why it lists memory over years, cumulative leakage and moving privacy-utility balances as open problems (Evaluations and benchmarks). CIMemories is the benchmark closest to the first two, inside the limits above.

Check yourself

  1. Which of the report's five steps does CIMemories measure, and which does it supply from outside the model?
    Answer

    It measures step 3, apply: does the model's message respect a norm it was never told? Step 2, locate, is supplied by the benchmark's labels, which GPT-5 produced as three personas. Steps 1, 4 and 5 are not exercised: the flow is given by the task, ambiguous pairs are dropped, and nothing is actually sent.

  2. Why does Appendix D code the benchmark's transmission principles as "Simple" even though the flow explorer offers four?
    Answer

    In the benchmark every message travels under the same principle: share what this task needs, at the user's request. The explorer's other principles (in confidence, volunteered, sold) are the theory's, not the benchmark's. Only the recipient and purpose vary in CIMemories.

  3. A product team trains a filter on CIMemories-style labels. In the report's vocabulary, what has it built?
    Answer

    A policy generator over aggregated preferences: the unanimous view of three privacy personas, produced by a model. The report's policy engine is meant to look up a context's norms, which are legitimate when they serve the context's purposes. Where the personas agree the two likely coincide; where they would disagree, the benchmark has no label, so the filter has no opinion.
