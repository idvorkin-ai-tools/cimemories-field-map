Contextual integrity says a flow of information is appropriate when it matches the norms of the context it happens in. Whether the information is secret has nothing to do with it. Read top to bottom; the first norm that matches the flow decides it. If none matches, nothing in this context sanctions the flow, and under contextual integrity that makes it a violation. This list is the piece nobody has for real contexts: a repository of norms an agent could look up is itself an open problem in the CAPS report (OP 6.3, OP 12.2); here the map wrote twelve by hand for three contexts.

    How CIMemories uses this

    In CIMemories four of the five parameters are fixed. The sender is always the assistant writing for the user, the subject is always the user, the information type is one remembered attribute, and the transmission principle is the same for every flow: the message shares what the task needs, at the user's request. Only the task varies, and it brings the recipient and the purpose. That is where the explorer above and the benchmark part ways: the principle chips you can flip up there (in confidence, volunteered, sold) are the theory's, and the benchmark never varies them, which is why the CAPS report's survey of benchmarks codes its transmission principles as "Simple" (bridge page). So every (attribute, task) pair gets its own label, and the label is a norm judgment made once, outside the model.

    A handful of decisions is enough to see the paper's idea: no row of your grid wants to be all green or all red, so no fixed "private" flag on a fact can be right. GPT-5 answers the same question for about 147 facts and 46 tasks per person, ten times as each of three personas, and a pair is scored only when all thirty answers agree. The model under test then has to get those cells right without ever seeing them. Revealing a withhold cell counts toward Violation@n, and leaving out a share cell costs completeness (Part 06).
